What the tests say

Every rewrite compiled and passed two independent fidelity audits. A reader model that can only follow what is written understood the rewrites better: {{N_O}} of comprehension questions right from the originals, {{N_B}} from a one-shot rewrite, {{N_L}} from the longest variant, {{N_C}} from a rewrite held to about the original's length. Other results were flat, and one runs against the plan.

  • Finding 1.The gain is in step-level understanding. On "why does this step hold" questions the weakest reader went from 51% to 73–79%. Three judge models preferred every rewrite to every original, in both presentation orders.
  • Finding 2.Readers that can reconstruct missing steps did not benefit. Haiku 5.5 at low effort scored 88% on the originals and 86–90% on the rewrites. Under a 1,500-word reading budget the gains mostly vanish: the weakest reader gained nothing, and the one reliable gain was 5 points for Haiku 5.5 on the plain compact variant.
  • Finding 3.Better exposition did not make an unflagged error easier to catch. With the same planted error in both versions, detection was 81% vs 88% for Haiku 5.5 and 69% vs 58% for Sonnet 5.5. Neither difference is reliable.
  • Finding 4.The rewriter itself was the effective checker. Given a manuscript with one planted error and told nothing, it flagged the error in 8 of 8 cases, and raised no false flag about internal mathematics on the eight clean papers.
  • Finding 5.Locating hard spots with weak readers helped only by adding words. Without a length cap it beat one-shot by 7 points at 27% more text and twice the cost. Under a length cap it made no measurable difference.

When the error goes through the rewriter blind

One corrupted original per paper was rewritten by the one-shot pipeline, which was told the source was believed correct. All eight rewrites carry an "Unverified step" box at the planted error, usually with the corrected statement. Readers of those rewrites were then asked whether the original's proof had an error.

Where this pushes back on the plan

  • Weaker reader models are not weak in the way the plan needs. Haiku 5.5 reconstructs what the text omits, so its score barely moves when the text improves. A model with reasoning off responds to the text but catches 0% of planted errors. No single reader gave a signal that tracks both comprehension and error-catching.
  • Self-reported difficulty is not a score. Weak readers always list about ten stuck points, nearly all minor. The count went from 13.1 to 11.3 after a rewrite and never approaches zero. It is useful as edit suggestions only.
  • Multiple-choice tests fail. Readers scored 95–100% without the paper. Short free-response questions with a closed-book check fixed that.
  • Models can now find the errors. The premise that models cannot locate their own mistakes dates from 2023 models. Here the rewriter found every planted error unprompted.
  • Legible is not the same as checkable. Sonnet's detection of unflagged errors dropped on the longer rewrites. It is not statistically established, but it matches human studies in which fluent explanations raise confidence without raising accuracy. The human study should test for it.

How it was done

  1. Rewrite. Claude Fable 5.2 rewrites the flattened LaTeX against a fixed spec: state the result and a "proof at a glance" first, give every proof as justified steps, keep all statements and the proof strategy, and box any step it cannot verify.
  2. Locate (two variants). Three Haiku 5.5 readers report where they got stuck. Their reports go to the rewriter, and in the "located" variant they re-read the draft for a second round.
  3. Audit. A fresh Fable context compares every statement with the source and checks added material; problems are repaired and re-audited. Opus 5.5 audits the result again.
  4. Evaluate. Quiz written by Opus from the original; errors planted by Opus and validated by Fable; answers graded blind by Sonnet and Opus. Readers were never used as graders of their own answers.

Limits

  • Every reader, judge, grader and auditor is a Claude model, as is the rewriter. Same-family preference may inflate the pairwise result.
  • Eight short, accessible papers. Intervals are wide and the release's 100-page manuscripts are a different problem.
  • Fidelity rests on two model audits. No person and no Lean check has confirmed the rewrites.
  • Planted errors are synthetic. Moving one into a rewrite means writing a flawed justification, which may change how detectable it is.

What I would do next

  1. Run a small human pilot before any training: compact + reports against the original, fixed time, step questions and one unflagged planted error. It also shows whether any reader model predicts human scores.
  2. Give flags types ("source error, corrected here" and "depends on an unchecked external result") and put a trust summary on page one.
  3. Check each rewrite's theorem against the Lean statement, and mine the Lean proof for steps the paper skips.
  4. Replace self-reported stuck points with step-justification probes, and spend the length budget where the weakest reader fails.
